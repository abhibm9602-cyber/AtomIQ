# Signal Processing and Spectroscopy Techniques Reference

## 1. Mathematical Foundations
### Fourier Transforms
The continuous Fourier Transform (FT) translates a signal from the time domain (t) to the frequency domain (omega):
X(omega) = integral_{-infty}^{+infty} x(t) * exp(-i * omega * t) dt
Inverse FT: x(t) = (1/2*pi) * integral_{-infty}^{+infty} X(omega) * exp(i * omega * t) d(omega)

### Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) and FFT
For sampled signals x[n] of length N:
X[k] = sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n] * exp(-i * 2*pi * k * n / N)
The Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) algorithm (e.g., Cooley-Tukey) reduces the computational complexity of DFT from O(N^2) to O(N log N).

### Power Spectral Density (PSD)
PSD characterises the power distribution of a signal across frequencies.
For a wide-sense stationary random process, the PSD S_xx(f) is the Fourier transform of its autocorrelation function R_xx(tau) (Wiener-Khinchin theorem):
S_xx(f) = integral_{-infty}^{+infty} R_xx(tau) * exp(-i * 2*pi * f * tau) d(tau)
Estimated using periodograms: S_xx(f) ~ (1/N) * |X(f)|^2

## 2. Noise Analysis in Physical Measurements
- White Noise: Flat PSD across all frequencies (e.g., thermal Johnson-Nyquist noise). S_V = 4*k_B*T*R.
- 1/f Noise (Flicker Noise): PSD proportional to 1/f^alpha (alpha ~ 1). Ubiquitous in electronics, dominant at low frequencies. Caused by trapping/detrapping or resistance fluctuations.
- Shot Noise: Arises from the discrete quantization of electric charge. S_I = 2*q*I, where q is elementary charge and I is average DC current. Flat spectrum up to very high frequencies.

### Lock-In Amplification
A technique to extract a known signal from extremely high noise environments.
Relies on phase-sensitive detection. The input signal (modulated at frequency f_ref) is multiplied by a reference signal at the same frequency:
V_out = (1/T) * integral_0^T V_in(t) * V_ref(t) dt
The multiplier output contains a DC component proportional to the signal amplitude and phase difference, while all noise at other frequencies averages to zero (low-pass filtered).

## 3. Structural and Elemental Characterization
### X-Ray Diffraction (XRD)
Probes crystal structure by elastic scattering of X-rays. Constructive interference is governed by Bragg's Law:
2*d * sin(theta) = n * lambda
where d is interplanar spacing, theta is incidence angle, lambda is X-ray wavelength (e.g., Cu K-alpha = 1.5406 Angstroms).
Rietveld refinement is a computational technique used to fit entire powder diffraction patterns to crystal models to determine precise atomic positions and phase fractions.

### X-Ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS)
Measures elemental composition and chemical states via the photoelectric effect.
Binding Energy (BE) = E_photon - Kinetic_Energy - Work_Function.
Chemical shifts in BE (e.g., shifts in C1s or O1s peaks) indicate oxidation states and bonding environments. Survey scans identify elements; high-resolution core-level scans resolve chemical states.

## 4. Optical and Vibrational Spectroscopy
### Raman Spectroscopy
Based on inelastic scattering of monochromatic light (laser).
- Stokes shift: Photon loses energy to create a phonon (vibrational mode). Resulting photon has lower frequency.
- Anti-Stokes shift: Photon gains energy by absorbing a phonon. Resulting photon has higher frequency.
Selection rules dictate that Raman active modes require a change in molecular polarizability. Distinct Raman peaks identify specific chemical bonds and crystal symmetries (e.g., G and 2D peaks in graphene).

### UV-Vis Spectroscopy
Measures optical absorption or transmission in the ultraviolet-visible range.
Absorption coefficient alpha can be used to determine the optical band gap E_g using a Tauc plot:
(alpha * h * nu)^(1/n) = A * (h * nu - E_g)
where h*nu is photon energy, A is a constant. n=1/2 for direct allowed transitions, n=2 for indirect allowed transitions. E_g is found by extrapolating the linear region to the x-axis.

## 5. Electrical Characterization
### Impedance Spectroscopy (EIS)
Applies a small AC voltage V(t) = V_0*sin(omega*t) and measures the current I(t) = I_0*sin(omega*t - phi).
Complex impedance Z(omega) = V/I = |Z| * exp(i*phi) = Z' + i*Z''
- Nyquist plot: -Z'' (imaginary part) vs Z' (real part) parameterized by frequency.
Data is fitted to Equivalent Circuit Models (ECMs) comprising resistors (R), capacitors (C), inductors (L), and Constant Phase Elements (CPEs) to extract physical parameters (e.g., bulk resistance, grain boundary capacitance).

### Current-Voltage (I-V) Characterization
Typically performed using a Source Measure Unit (SMU) like Keithley instruments.
- Sweep measurements: Ramping voltage and measuring current.
- Compliance limits MUST be set to prevent device breakdown or thermal runaway (e.g., setting I_compliance = 1 mA during an RRAM forming step).
Parameters extracted include resistance, leakage current, breakdown voltage, and threshold voltage.

## 6. Microscopy Techniques
### Electron Microscopy (SEM / TEM)
- Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM): Scans a focused electron beam over the surface. Secondary Electrons (SE) give topography; Backscattered Electrons (BSE) give compositional contrast. Resolution ~1-10 nm.
- Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM): Beam passes through an ultra-thin sample (<100 nm). Enables atomic resolution (<0.1 nm). Can perform Electron Diffraction (SAED) for crystallography and Electron Energy Loss Spectroscopy (EELS) / Energy-Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDX) for elemental mapping.

### Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM)
Uses a sharp probe on a microcantilever to map surface topography.
- Contact mode: Tip drags across the surface.
- Tapping mode (AC mode): Cantilever oscillates near resonance; amplitude changes due to tip-sample interactions map topography without damaging soft samples.
- Force-distance curves measure adhesion, stiffness, and nanoscale mechanical properties.
